Sample AI outputs (Chapter 4, Generative AI & Prompting). TEACHER KEY: every claim in genai-sample-outputs.txt
marked supported / uncertain / wrong, with the source to check it against, plus the planted issues in
genai-red-team-prompts.txt. This key lives in docs/teacher and is never deployed with the site.

Both answers are synthetic. They were written for this course in the style of a chatbot; no AI tool produced them.
The question both times: Tell me about the River Shannon.

=== Answer 1 (first run): 7 supported, 2 uncertain, 3 wrong ===
1. [SUPPORTED] The River Shannon is the longest river in Ireland.
   Check: Any atlas; Tailte Éireann, formerly Ordnance Survey Ireland (tailte.ie); Wikipedia "River Shannon" and the sources it cites.
2. [SUPPORTED] It rises at the Shannon Pot on the slopes of Cuilcagh Mountain in County Cavan.
   Check: Geological Survey Ireland (gsi.ie) on the Shannon Pot; Cuilcagh Lakelands Geopark; Tailte Éireann map.
3. [WRONG] From there it flows south for nearly 500 km before reaching the Atlantic.
   Check: Every reference gives about 360 km (360.5 km on Wikipedia and Tailte Éireann; some older sources 386 km including the estuary). No source gives 500 km.
4. [SUPPORTED] On its way it widens into three large lakes: Lough Allen, Lough Ree and Lough Derg.
   Check: Tailte Éireann map; Waterways Ireland Shannon Navigation guide.
5. [UNCERTAIN] The river passes through or borders eleven counties, dividing the west of Ireland from the east.
   Check: Sources count differently depending on whether estuary counties and county borders are included; check the list against an Tailte Éireann map rather than trusting the number.
6. [SUPPORTED] The city of Limerick sits at the head of the Shannon Estuary.
   Check: Tailte Éireann map; Limerick City and County Council.
7. [SUPPORTED] In 1925 the new Irish state began building the Ardnacrusha hydroelectric scheme, which was completed in 1929.
   Check: ESB Archives (esbarchives.ie) Shannon Scheme pages: construction 1925–1929, official opening July 1929.
8. [SUPPORTED] The scheme was run by the Electricity Supply Board, set up in 1927.
   Check: ESB Archives; Electricity (Supply) Act 1927 on irishstatutebook.ie.
9. [UNCERTAIN] For its first decade Ardnacrusha supplied about 80% of Ireland's electricity.
   Check: Widely repeated, but the share fell every year as demand grew, so any percentage needs a year attached. Check the ESB Archives (esbarchives.ie) Shannon Scheme pages for dated figures.
10. [WRONG] The Shannon Bridge Act of 1873 required every crossing of the river to be approved by Parliament.
   Check: No such Act exists. The nearest real Acts on legislation.gov.uk are the Shannon Navigation Acts (1839 onward) and the Shannon Act 1874, which deal with navigation and drainage works and say nothing about Parliament approving crossings. That is the tell: a plausible name, a nearby year, an invented provision.
11. [SUPPORTED] The river is named after Sionann, a figure from Irish mythology.
   Check: Placenames Database of Ireland (logainm.ie) entry for the Shannon; any dictionary of Irish mythology.
12. [WRONG] For a fuller history see Ó Braonáin, T. (2011), The Shannon from Pot to Sea, Athlone Riverside Press, p. 42.
   Check: Fabricated citation. No such author, title or publisher in the National Library of Ireland catalogue, WorldCat or any bookshop search.

=== Answer 2 (second run, different model): 5 supported, 4 uncertain, 2 wrong ===
1. [SUPPORTED] The Shannon is the longest river on the island of Ireland at about 360 km.
   Check: OSI; Wikipedia "River Shannon" (360.5 km).
2. [UNCERTAIN] Some references give 386 km, a figure that includes the estuary.
   Check: Older references do give 386 km; whether that includes the estuary depends on the reference. The point is that "the length" depends on where you say the river ends.
3. [SUPPORTED] Its source, the Shannon Pot, is a small pool in County Cavan fed by underground streams from Cuilcagh Mountain.
   Check: Geological Survey Ireland; Cuilcagh Lakelands Geopark.
4. [UNCERTAIN] The river drains roughly one fifth of the island.
   Check: The Shannon catchment is often given as about a fifth of the island; the exact share depends on how the catchment is defined. Check the EPA catchment data (catchments.ie).
5. [SUPPORTED] Major towns along it include Carrick-on-Shannon, Athlone and Limerick.
   Check: Tailte Éireann map.
6. [WRONG] The Ardnacrusha power station, built by the German firm Siemens-Schuckert, opened in 1937.
   Check: The firm is right; the year is wrong. ESB Archives: construction 1925–1929, official opening July 1929.
7. [UNCERTAIN] At the time it was one of the largest hydroelectric schemes in the world.
   Check: Often described that way, but "largest" claims need a stated comparison and date. Check the ESB Archives Shannon Scheme pages for what they actually say.
8. [SUPPORTED] The Shannon–Erne Waterway, which links the river to Lough Erne, was reopened in 1994.
   Check: Waterways Ireland; the restored canal opened in 1994.
9. [SUPPORTED] The river also gives its name to Shannon Airport, where the world's first airport duty-free shop opened in 1947.
   Check: Shannon Airport history pages; widely documented, opened 1947.
10. [WRONG] Because the Shannon is so slow and flat, it has never flooded seriously.
   Check: Major floods in November 2009 and winter 2015–16 are documented by the Office of Public Works (floodinfo.ie) and in national news archives.
11. [UNCERTAIN] Salmon still migrate up the river, though numbers are lower than in the past.
   Check: Inland Fisheries Ireland publishes counts; "lower than in the past" needs a date range and a figure before it can be used.

Points worth drawing out in discussion:
- The two answers disagree on the length (nearly 500 km vs about 360 km) and on the Ardnacrusha year (1929 vs 1937)
  in the same confident tone. Neither answer flags the disagreement; only checking finds it.
- The fabricated citation in Answer 1 (Ó Braonáin, 2011) looks exactly like a real one. A library catalogue search
  finds nothing. That is the myth-buster "If it gives a citation, the source exists" in one line.
- The uncertain claims are not wrong so much as unfinished: "about 80%", "one fifth", "eleven counties" all need a
  year, a definition or a list before they can be used. "Uncertain" is the right verdict, and the change to make is
  a hedge or a question mark, not deletion.

=== Red-team prompt cards: planted issues ===
Prompt 1: Write a short piece for the school newsletter about the history of our town, and include the year it was founded and its population.
- Ambiguity: "our town": the model does not know which town, so it will pick a likely one or invent a generic history. "Short" is undefined.
- Missing constraint: No audience or level, no word limit, and no "say if unsure", so the model fills every gap with its most average guess.
- Claim to check: The founding year and the population figure. The model will supply both confidently whether or not any record exists.

Prompt 2: Explain why electric cars are better for the environment, with some statistics to back it up.
- Ambiguity: "Better" than what: petrol cars, buses, bicycles? And where: emissions depend on how a country generates its electricity.
- Missing constraint: No country, no year, no requirement to name the source of each statistic, and no length.
- Claim to check: Every statistic. The framing is also leading ("why is X better"), so expect a one-sided answer with no drawbacks.

Prompt 3: Make me a two-week revision plan for my exams with the best memory techniques, backed by research.
- Ambiguity: Which subjects, how many exams, and how many hours a day are available? The plan will be for an imaginary student.
- Missing constraint: No format (a table by day?), no level, and nothing about what to avoid (for example, no all-night sessions).
- Claim to check: "Backed by research": any named study, author or percentage the model attaches to a technique needs checking. Citations here are often invented.
