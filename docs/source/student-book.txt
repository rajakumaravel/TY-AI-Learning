How to use this book
This isn't a book about AI that you read and then get tested on. It's a set of eight
missions. In each one you'll use a real tool, try to break it, measure what happened
and decide what you think. You'll spend most of your time doing, not listening.
Every mission follows the same rhythm
MISSION PREDICT DISCOVER TRY TEST IMPROVE REFLECT EVIDENCE
…then the next mission begins
The learning loop. You'll see it in every chapter.
You'll predict what will happen before you try anything, because being wrong on purpose is the
fastest way to learn. You'll test rather than trust. And at the end you'll keep evidence: a map, a
log, a rewrite, a prototype. That evidence builds up into your portfolio across the 30 hours.

The one rule that runs through everything
AI outputs are guesses until you check them
Every answer an AI gives you is a hypothesis. It might be right. It might be confidently
wrong. You are responsible for what you hand in, say or build, not the tool. When
something matters, verify it against a source that doesn't come from an AI.
Keeping yourself and others safe
Never type your own or anyone else's private details (full name, address, health, passwords,
photos of people) into an AI tool. Use made-up examples instead.
Only use tools your school has approved. If you're not sure, ask.
You can always choose not to use AI for a task. That's a valid choice, and sometimes the
better one.
Some topics in this book (bias, misinformation, the future of work) can be uncomfortable.
It's fine to say so, and to disagree with each other respectfully.
How you'll know you're doing well
There are no marks out of 100 here. Each chapter ends with a How am I doing? box with three
levels: Getting started, Getting there and Going further. Be honest with yourself. Moving from
one column to the next is the goal. You'll also earn a badge for each chapter you complete.
Mistakes and failed models count as evidence. A model that fails in an interesting way teaches
you more than one that "just works".
Your portfolio
Throughout the book, the For your portfolio box lists what to keep. Blank sheets for the most
common pieces of evidence are at the back (Portfolio sheets). Every piece of evidence should
show four things: what you predicted, what you tried, what actually happened, and what you'd
change.

You're going to teach a computer to tell two objects apart, then do everything you can
to confuse it. Machine learning is an experiment, not magic. You'll create labelled
examples, train a small model, test it on things it has never seen, count the mistakes,
and then discover how a model can cheat without anyone telling it to.
By the end of this chapter you will be able to…
Explain labels, features, training data, test data and prediction.
Train a basic classifier using an approved visual tool or a simulator your teacher provides.
Record predictions and work out simple accuracy.
Read a basic confusion matrix.
Explain how training data that doesn't represent the real world creates poor or biased
results.
Key words
Supervised learning – teaching a model
with examples that already have the right
answer attached
Class label – the category an example
belongs to, e.g. “cup” or “bottle”
Feature – something measurable about an
example: colour, shape, edges, brightness
Training data – the examples the model
learns from
Test data – new examples used to check
the model; should not be reused from
training
Model – the trained system that makes
predictions
Prediction – the model's guess for a new
example
Accuracy – correct predictions ÷ total
predictions
Confusion matrix – a table showing which
classes got mixed up with which
Generalisation – how well the model
handles examples it wasn't trained on
Shortcut learning – when the model relies
on an easy, unintended clue (like
background colour) instead of the real
thing
Bias – a systematic skew that makes
results unfair or unrepresentative

Your mission

The route

Write a set of rules a stranger could follow to tell your two objects apart. Swap

Instead of writing every rule, machine learning gives the model lots of examples
with labels and lets it find the features that separate them. It learns statistical
patterns. Nobody types “cups have handles”.

The shortcut experiment
Now deliberately build a flawed dataset. Put almost all of Class A on a light
background and almost all of Class B on a dark background. Train. Then test with
the backgrounds swapped. Did the model learn the object, or did it learn the
background? This is shortcut learning, and it is how real systems end up biased
without anyone intending it.

Data is the raw material of AI. Every model in this book, and every app on your phone,
runs on it. This chapter is an investigation: what does a service actually collect, what
does it work out about you without you telling it, and who gets left out when a dataset
is built? You'll finish by designing a better data plan than the one you started with.
By the end of this chapter you will be able to…
Tell apart volunteered, observed and inferred data.
Investigate how a digital service may collect and use data.
Explain why purpose, minimisation and representativeness matter.
Describe what GDPR and the EU AI Act mean for someone your age.
Spot a dataset that could leave people out or misrepresent them.
Key words
Personal data – any information about a
person who could be identified from it
Volunteered data – information you give
on purpose
Observed data – information recorded
while you use something
Inferred data – information a system
guesses about you from other data
Cookies – small files a website stores on
your device to remember or track you
Tracking – following what you do across
pages, apps or time
Web scraping – automatically copying data
from public web pages
Public / private data – whether data is
openly available or restricted; “public”
doesn't mean “free to use for anything”

genuinely needed for a stated purpose
Consent – agreeing to a use of your data,
freely and with real understanding
GDPR – the EU's data protection law; gives
people rights over their data
EU AI Act – an EU law that regulates AI by
how risky its use is
Representativeness – whether a dataset
actually reflects all the people it will affect

20–40 min Discover: what an LLM actually does
An LLM has read an enormous amount of text and learned to predict what usually
comes next. When you ask a question, it generates a likely continuation. That's
why it's fluent. It's also why it's not a database of checked facts. It can produce a
smooth, confident paragraph about something that never happened. Confident
wording is not confident truth. Keep that sentence. You'll need it.

Four useful roles
Try the AI in four different jobs. Each has a different prompt style and a different
risk.
Tutor: “Explain X to me, then quiz me.” Risk: it explains something wrong,
fluently.
Brainstorm partner: “Give me 10 unusual angles on X.” Risk: you stop
generating your own.

Glossary
Plain meanings for the words in this book. Chapter key-word boxes give the short
version; this is the fuller one.
AGI Artificial General Intelligence. A hypothetical AI with broad, general
capability across many kinds of task. Not the same as today's
specialised systems.
Algorithm A set of steps or rules for turning an input into an output.
AI model A system trained or designed to recognise patterns, generate
outputs or make predictions.
Automation bias Trusting a machine's output more than the evidence justifies, just
because a machine produced it.
Bias A systematic skew that leads to unfair, unrepresentative or
misleading results.
Class label The category attached to an example in supervised learning, e.g.
“cup”.
Confusion matrix A table comparing what was actually true with what the model
predicted, so the different kinds of error can be seen.
Data minimisation Collecting only the data genuinely needed for a stated purpose.
Deep learning Machine learning that uses multi-layer neural networks. Mentioned
in this book only as an idea.
Disclosure Saying clearly when and how AI helped with a piece of work.
Generalisation A model's ability to perform sensibly on examples it never saw
during training.
Generative AI AI that creates new content such as text, images, audio or code
from learned patterns.
Hallucination A fluent, confident output that is unsupported, wrong or made up.
Human-in-the-loop A workflow where a person reviews, decides or approves at
important points.
Inferred data Information predicted about a person or situation from other data,
rather than provided directly.
Large language model (LLM) A generative model trained on huge amounts of text to predict and
generate language.
Lateral reading Checking a source by leaving it and finding out what other,
independent sources say.
Machine learning Methods where a system learns patterns from data instead of being
given every rule explicitly.

===== PAGE 46 =====
Narrow AI AI built for a specific task or family of tasks. All the AI in this book.
Prompt The instructions and context given to a generative AI system.
Provenance Where a piece of content came from and how it reached you.
Red-teaming Deliberately trying to make your own system fail or misbehave, to
find weaknesses before someone else does.
Shortcut learning When a model relies on an easy, unintended clue (like background
colour) instead of the feature you meant it to learn.
Test data Examples used to evaluate a model after training. Ideally never
reused from the training set.
Training data Examples used to teach a machine-learning model.
Verification Checking a claim against independent, reliable evidence.

About this programme
AI in Practice is a 30-hour Transition Year programme built around active, inquiry-based and
self-directed learning. It is designed so that you learn AI by using it, testing it, questioning it
and building with it. Passive instruction is kept deliberately short.
What you're working towards
The programme is aligned to the nine learning outcomes of the NCCA/CSLINC Exploring
Artificial Intelligence Transition Year micro-module. Each chapter opener tells you which ones
it links to.
Outcome You should be able to…
LO1 Outline fundamental concepts of artificial intelligence.
LO2 Use and develop a basic AI model to perform tasks.
LO3 Evaluate AI models using standard metrics.
LO4 Examine the positive and negative potential of AI models.
LO5 Investigate how AI developers gather and collect data.
LO6 Examine ethical considerations around data used for AI.
LO7 Explore generative AI capabilities, particularly in an educational context.
LO8 Discuss possible AI futures, with emphasis on societal impacts.
LO9 Investigate misinformation and bias in AI use and ways to reduce their impact.

What this programme deliberately avoids
Long slide-led lectures on AI terminology.
Teaching prompting as “secret phrases” to memorise.
Treating AI output as authoritative evidence.
Requiring coding from every student.
Using grades as the main motivator.
Mistaking a polished prototype for deep learning.
