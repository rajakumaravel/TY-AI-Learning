# AI in Practice — Comprehensive Curriculum Review and Data Integrity Plan (recommendations for v1.1). Text extracted from the docx; one line per paragraph or table cell.

AI in Practice
Comprehensive Curriculum Review,Data Integrity Enhancement Plan & Revision Blueprint
Review of Student Book · Pilot Edition · Version 1.0 · September 2026
Purpose
To identify curriculum strengths, technical and pedagogical gaps, data-integrity risks, assessment issues and opportunities for a stronger Version 1.1/2.0, while preserving the book's practical, student-centred character.
Executive Summary
The Student Book has a strong pedagogical identity. It consistently asks learners to use AI, test it, question it, improve it and preserve evidence rather than treating AI as authoritative. Its mission-based design, emphasis on experimentation, visible human oversight, bias awareness, verification and reflection are all significant strengths. The strongest design decision is that failure is treated as evidence: students are encouraged to break models, identify uncertainty and explain what did not work.
Source anchor: pp. 3–4: the recurring MISSION → PREDICT → DISCOVER → TRY → TEST → IMPROVE → REFLECT → EVIDENCE loop; students are told to verify AI outputs and preserve evidence.
The principal weakness is not that data is ignored; data is actually central to Chapters 2, 3, 6 and 8. The weakness is that the curriculum largely treats data through the lenses of collection, privacy, representativeness and responsible use. It does not yet give students a systematic method for establishing whether data are accurate, complete, consistent, valid, unique, current, traceable and fit for purpose. In other words, the book needs a formal data-integrity and data-quality strand.
The recommended revision is therefore not to replace the existing structure. It is to add a cross-curricular 'trust the data only after checking the data' discipline that parallels the book's existing 'trust AI outputs only after checking them' discipline.

Recommended revision philosophy
Preserve the practical, inquiry-based structure. Do not turn the programme into a lecture-heavy data-science textbook.
Teach every new concept through a visible action: inspect, compare, calculate, trace, clean, challenge, document or justify.
Make data integrity a repeated habit across Chapters 2, 3, 6 and 8 rather than a single isolated definition.
Separate what is known, observed, inferred, generated, estimated and assumed.
Make evidence quality assessable: students should earn credit for documenting uncertainty and limitations, not merely for producing a successful prototype.
Use simple language first, with professional terminology introduced as an optional or secondary label where useful.

2. Data Integrity: The Major Curriculum Gap
Chapter 3 opens with the correct premise that data is the raw material of AI. It then focuses on data collection categories, inference, minimisation, consent, representativeness, GDPR, public/private data and responsible use. Those topics are important, but a learner can complete the chapter without having to demonstrate that a dataset itself is trustworthy.
Source anchor: pp. 14–17: Chapter 3 learning outcomes and Responsible Data focus.
The document does not currently use 'data integrity' as an explicit term. The word 'quality' appears in relation to source quality rather than as a systematic framework for data quality. Provenance is defined in relation to content provenance. This provides a strong foundation, but it leaves an important technical gap.
2.1 Proposed student-facing definition
DATA INTEGRITY: Confidence that data remains accurate, complete, consistent, valid and traceable from the point it is collected through the point it is analysed or used by an AI system.
For Transition Year, the definition should be accompanied by a memorable question: "Can I trust this data enough for this purpose, and can I show why?"

4. Chapter 2 – Teaching a Machine: Detailed Revision
Chapter 2 already contains real machine-learning practice: labelled examples, train/test separation, accuracy, a confusion matrix, shortcut learning, a deliberate failure experiment, retraining and comparison of model versions. These features should be retained.
Source anchor: pp. 9–13: supervised learning concepts, 10-row test log, confusion matrix, shortcut experiment, v1/v2 comparison; precision/recall currently optional.
4.1 Add label integrity and 'ground truth'
The current cup/bottle task makes labels look unambiguous. In many real systems, the label is itself a human judgement. Students should experience disagreement before being told that labels are 'truth'.
Give each group 15–20 examples containing a few deliberately ambiguous cases.
Have two students label the same examples independently.
Calculate simple percentage agreement.
Discuss why disagreement occurred.
Ask which labels should be considered ground truth and who has authority to decide.
Record disagreements rather than hiding them.
Key teaching question: If humans disagree about the correct label, what exactly is the machine being trained to learn?
4.2 Add data leakage
The book correctly warns that test data should be genuinely different from training data. The next step is to name the failure mode: data leakage.
DATA LEAKAGE: Information from the test set, future data or the target answer accidentally reaches the training process, making performance look better than it would be in real use.
Near-duplicate photos from the same burst are split between train and test.
Students repeatedly modify the training set after examining exactly which final test images failed.
A field that directly reveals the answer is accidentally included as a feature.
Future information is used to predict an earlier outcome.
4.3 Clarify train, validation and test
Set
Plain-language purpose
When students use it
Training
The examples the model is allowed to learn from.
During model building.
Validation
Examples used while making improvement choices.
During model v1 → v2 iteration.
Final test
Examples kept aside for the final honest check.
Only after the improvement decisions are finished.
The classroom activity can remain simpler if necessary, but the text should note that repeatedly tuning against one test set weakens its value as a final independent evaluation.
4.4 Add baseline performance
Accuracy becomes far more meaningful when compared with a baseline. A classic classroom example is an imbalanced dataset with 90 cats and 10 dogs. A useless classifier that predicts 'cat' every time achieves 90% accuracy. This instantly demonstrates why a percentage alone cannot establish quality.
4.5 Reframe the 10-row test log
Ten examples are appropriate for teaching the mechanics of a confusion matrix, but not for claiming that a model has a stable real-world accuracy. Add this exact warning:
Ten test cases are enough to learn the method. They are not enough to prove that a real AI system is reliable. Write '8 of our 10 tests were correct' rather than assuming the model will always be 80% accurate.
4.6 Make recall conceptually core
Precision and recall currently appear as an optional level-up task. Because LO3 concerns standard model evaluation metrics, recall should at least be mandatory conceptually, even if the formula remains optional.
Recall question: Of all the real positive cases, how many did the model find?
Precision question: Of everything the model flagged, how much was actually positive?
Application question: Which error is more serious in this context, and why?
5. Chapter 3 – Data Detective: Major Redesign Opportunity
This is the natural home for the strongest data-integrity additions. The existing privacy, consent, representativeness and rights material should remain, but the mission should require students to audit both what data are collected and whether those data are trustworthy.
5.1 Revised learning outcomes
Distinguish volunteered, observed and inferred data.
Explain the purpose of data minimisation and representativeness.
Check a small dataset for accuracy, completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness and timeliness.
Trace the source and transformations of data using a simple provenance record.
Explain why missingness, duplicates, labels and outdated data can distort AI or analysis.
Create a Responsible Data & Integrity Card that documents privacy, quality, representation and governance risks.
Explain what conclusions the dataset can and cannot support.
5.2 Add a 30-minute Data Integrity Lab
Provide a fictional spreadsheet with 25–40 rows containing deliberately planted problems:
two duplicate rows;
one impossible age;
one date in a different format;
three missing values;
inconsistent category spelling;
mixed kilometres and miles;
one legitimate extreme outlier;
one record with an uncertain label;
one outdated record;
a column that is unnecessary for the stated purpose.
Students should not immediately edit the sheet. First they profile it, identify issues and decide whether each issue should be corrected, removed, retained, standardised or investigated.
5.3 Teach missing data
Missing values are particularly important because absence itself may be patterned. Avoid heavy statistical terminology, but make students ask: 'Is this missing by accident, or are certain people more likely to be missing?'
Example: If a transport survey is completed mainly through a smartphone app, people without reliable smartphones may be missing for a reason. The missingness can therefore distort the result.
5.4 Teach duplicates and identity matching
Students should see that uniqueness is not always obvious. 'Aoife Murphy', 'A. Murphy' and 'Aoife M Murphy' may represent one person or three different people. The exercise should teach caution: suspected duplicates should be investigated, not automatically deleted.
5.5 Teach outliers correctly
An outlier is not automatically an error. A 160 km commute in a dataset where most journeys are 1–20 km might be a typo, a real exceptional case or a unit problem. The correct first response is investigation.
Recommended rule: unusual data is evidence to investigate, not evidence to delete.
5.6 Teach metadata and a data dictionary
A value without context can be meaningless. Students should learn the word metadata and produce a small data dictionary.

7. Chapter 4 – Generative AI & Prompting: Add Transformation Integrity
Chapter 4 is strong on hallucinations, prompt iteration, source checking and confirmation bias. It also already notes that a transformer role can drop or change meaning. That point should be expanded into a formal data-integrity warning.
Source anchor: pp. 19–22: prompt iteration, verification, transformer risk, confirmation bias and source checking.
7.1 Add 'AI can corrupt data while helping'
Summarising a table may omit records or change quantities.
Categorising survey comments may merge distinct themes or invent a category.
Converting units may introduce arithmetic errors.
Extracting dates or names may silently misread text.
Cleaning data may replace legitimate rare values.
Filling blank fields may convert an estimate into something that looks observed.
Students should therefore compare the input and output of any AI-assisted transformation and verify counts, totals, categories and critical fields.

8.2 Add proxy variables
Students should learn that removing an explicitly sensitive field does not automatically remove its influence. Postcode, school, device type, language or travel pattern can act as proxies for other characteristics.
8.3 Add intersectional thinking
Looking at one characteristic at a time can conceal failures affecting people at the intersection of several groups. A system might perform acceptably for girls overall and for disabled students overall while still performing poorly for disabled girls. This can be introduced conceptually without requiring complex statistics.

10. Add Analytical Integrity, Not Just Data Integrity
Even perfectly accurate records can be analysed misleadingly. Students should therefore learn that integrity also applies to choices made after data collection.
Cherry-picking only results that support the preferred conclusion.
Changing the success measure after seeing the data.
Deleting inconvenient cases without a documented rule.
Using a dramatic chart scale to exaggerate a small difference.
Confusing correlation with causation.
Reporting percentages without the underlying counts.
Generalising from a tiny or unrepresentative sample.
Presenting an estimate or AI-generated value as observed fact.
10.1 Add correlation vs causation
This is a high-value general data-literacy addition. A simple rule is sufficient: two things moving together does not prove that one caused the other. Students should be asked to suggest alternative explanations and possible confounding factors.
10.2 Require counts with percentages
A statement such as '80% preferred the prototype' is much more informative when accompanied by '8 of 10 test users'. This prevents small samples from sounding more definitive than they are.
11. Governance, Privacy, Security and Retention
The book has a strong safety baseline: private details should not be entered into AI systems and schools should approve tools. Chapter 3 also notes accuracy, security, transparency, correction/deletion rights and retention choices. The next revision should make the operational controls visible.
Source anchor: p. 4: private-data and approved-tool rules; p. 16: accuracy/security/transparency and retention choices.
11.1 Distinguish privacy from security
Privacy asks…
Security asks…
Should we collect and use this personal information?
How do we prevent unauthorised access or alteration?
Is the use proportionate and transparent?
Who can read, edit, export or delete the data?
How long should the data be retained?
Where is it stored and what happens if access is compromised?
11.2 Student-facing governance controls
Access control: only people who need the data should have access.
Least privilege: editing rights should be narrower than viewing rights where possible.
Storage: know where project files are stored and which tools sync them elsewhere.
Credentials: never put passwords, API keys or access tokens into prompts or shared documents.
Version control: preserve the raw source and clearly label later versions.
Retention: define how long data are needed.
Deletion: consider copies, exports, screenshots and derived datasets, not only the original file.
Escalation: define what happens when a student discovers unsafe, sensitive or suspicious information.
11.3 Anonymisation vs pseudonymisation
A student identifier such as 'Participant 017' may hide a name but can still be linked back to a person if the key exists. That is better described as pseudonymisation. Truly anonymised information cannot reasonably be linked back to an individual. The book already correctly warns that combinations of seemingly anonymous attributes can re-identify people; this distinction would make that lesson more precise.
12. Expand Provenance into Data Lineage
Provenance is currently defined well for information and media. Extend the same habit to data and models.
Trace backward: OUTPUT → MODEL / ANALYSIS → PROCESSED DATA → RAW DATA → COLLECTION METHOD → ORIGINAL SOURCE.
For every arrow, students ask: What changed? Who changed it? When? Why? This creates a simple audit trail and supports reproducibility.

15. Research-with-People Safeguards for Chapter 8
The book already requires teacher-approved interview/survey questions and minimisation of personal data. A more explicit classroom protocol would improve consistency and safeguarding.
Participation should be voluntary.
Participants should know the purpose of the activity.
Students should not collect names unless genuinely necessary and approved.
No sensitive questions should be added informally.
No covert audio, photo, video or screen recording.
Participants should be able to stop without explanation.
Raw interview/survey material should not be pasted into public or unapproved AI tools.
Reports should use aggregate or de-identified results where possible.
A retention/deletion point should be agreed before collection begins.
Teachers should define the escalation route for disclosures of harm, safeguarding concerns or sensitive personal information.
These are curriculum recommendations for safe classroom practice; they are not a substitute for a school's own policies, data-protection procedures or legal guidance.
16. Clarify Model vs System vs Product
Term
Recommended explanation
Why the distinction matters
AI model
The trained component that produces a prediction or generated output.
A model can perform well while the wider system fails.
AI system
Model + data + rules + interface + human roles + monitoring.
Risk often arises outside the model itself.
AI-enabled product/service
The complete tool a user interacts with.
User experience, access, workflow and governance affect outcomes.
This distinction would strengthen the current emphasis on human oversight and help students understand why responsibility cannot be reduced to model accuracy.
17. Add Monitoring and Model/Data Drift
Evaluation should not stop when a prototype works once. Introduce the idea that the world can change and that a system's input patterns may change with it.
DRIFT: A change over time in the data, users or environment that can make an earlier model or rule work less well.
language and slang change;
consumer preferences change;
school processes change;
transport patterns change;
fraud tactics change;
device types or input quality change.
Chapter 8 presentations should include one question: 'If this ran for a year, what would we monitor?'
18. Accessibility and Digital Exclusion
The curriculum already asks who is excluded and gives examples such as people without smartphones. The revision should make accessibility a standard design check.
Can a user with low vision read the interface?

Confidence that data stays accurate, complete, consistent, valid and traceable as it is collected, changed and used.

How suitable and reliable data is for the job you want it to do.

Where data came from and what happened to it before you used it.

The path data follows from its source through cleaning, transformation, analysis or modelling.

When information that should be hidden during training accidentally influences the model and makes testing look better.

A record that represents the same real thing more than once.

Change over time that can reduce how well a model or rule works.

Replacing direct identifiers with codes while a link back to a person may still exist.

Artificially generated data designed to resemble real data.

25. Copy-Ready Student Text for a New Data Integrity Box
Before you trust the result, trust the data - and before you trust the data, check it.A dataset can look neat and still contain problems. Check whether values are accurate, whether important information is missing, whether the same thing is written consistently, whether values follow the rules, whether records appear more than once and whether the data is recent enough for the job. Then ask where it came from, what was changed and who might be missing.Never change a strange value simply because it is inconvenient. Investigate it. If you make a change, record what you changed and why. Keep the original data. Your goal is not to make the dataset look perfect; your goal is to make your decisions traceable.
26. Copy-Ready Student Text for AI-Assisted Data Work
AI can help you organise, summarise or transform data, but it can also silently change it. If an AI tool reformats a table, groups responses, fills blanks, converts units or extracts information, compare the result with the original. Check counts, totals and important values. Keep observed values separate from inferred, estimated or AI-generated values. AI assistance does not remove your responsibility for the final dataset.
27. Copy-Ready Student Text for Small Samples
Small tests are useful for learning, but they do not prove how a system will perform everywhere. If 8 out of 10 test users complete a task, report '8 of our 10 test users completed it' rather than claiming that 80% of all students will succeed. Always report the number of people or examples behind a percentage.
Conclusion
The Student Book already has the right educational philosophy for responsible AI: students test rather than trust, retain evidence, challenge outputs, identify bias, use human oversight, compare AI and non-AI approaches, and communicate uncertainty. The recommended revision does not require abandoning that approach. It requires applying the same scepticism to the underlying data.
The most important conceptual shift is simple: responsible AI education should teach three linked questions. First, can we trust the output? Second, can we trust the model or process that produced it? Third, can we trust the data on which that process depends? Version 1.0 handles the first question strongly and much of the second. A stronger Version 1.1/2.0 should make the third equally explicit.
If the proposed data-integrity strand, structured cleaning workflow, provenance records, subgroup evaluation, baseline comparison, stronger Chapter 8 assessment and governance controls are incorporated, the programme would move from a strong introductory AI-literacy workbook toward a notably more rigorous applied AI, data-literacy and responsible-technology curriculum while remaining accessible to Transition Year students.
