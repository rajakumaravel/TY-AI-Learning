PROGRAMME PROMISE
Students learn AI by using it, testing it, questioning it and building with it. Passive instruction is deliberately kept short; the majority of time is spent exploring, creating, discussing, evaluating and reflecting.
Designed for pilot delivery with Transition Year students. No authentication, LMS or platform dependency is assumed. Activities can be delivered using school-approved browser tools, paper worksheets and standard productivity software.

1. Programme overview
AI in Practice is a 30-hour Transition Year programme built around active, inquiry-based and self-directed learning. It is intentionally designed so that a teacher or facilitator does not need to deliver long technical lectures. Students encounter a challenge, receive a small amount of targeted explanation, use a real or simulated tool, test what happened, produce evidence and reflect.
Programme aims
Build a practical understanding of how contemporary AI systems work and fail.
Develop confident, responsible use of generative AI for learning and real-world tasks.
Strengthen data literacy, verification habits, bias awareness and digital judgement.
Develop self-directed learning, collaboration, creativity and communication through hands-on missions.
Connect AI literacy with careers, citizenship, ethics and future choices.
Integrate learning through a final innovation project grounded in a real problem.
Recommended audience and prerequisites
Designed for TY students, typically around ages 15-16. No previous coding, machine-learning or advanced mathematics knowledge is required. Students should have basic browser, file and document skills. Technical extensions are optional.

Schools may timetable the blocks as double periods, workshop days or a combination. The sequence is recommended but not rigid. Blocks 1-5 form the conceptual core; Blocks 6-8 deepen transfer, future thinking and project work.
Learning loop
EVERY MISSION FOLLOWS THE SAME RHYTHM
MISSION → PREDICT → DISCOVER → TRY → TEST → IMPROVE → REFLECT → EVIDENCE. Students should spend more time doing than listening.
What this curriculum deliberately avoids
Long slide-led lectures on AI terminology.
Teaching prompt engineering as “secret phrases” or formula memorisation.
Treating AI output as authoritative evidence.
Requiring coding for all students.
Using grades as the main motivator.
Equating a polished prototype with deep learning.
2. Curriculum alignment and learning outcomes
The curriculum is structured to align with the NCCA/CSLINC Exploring Artificial Intelligence Transition Year micro-module while extending it through applied digital skills and an 8-hour innovation project. The official micro-module identifies nine learning outcomes and is designed for a minimum of 20 hours of class contact time, with scope for self-directed independent learning. This pilot retains those core outcomes within a 30-hour applied experience.

3. Delivery model and facilitator principles
The facilitator role
The facilitator is a guide, questioner and safety guardrail rather than the source of every answer. In most activities, students should encounter the task before receiving the explanation. When students struggle, use staged hints: ask a question first, give a clue second, explain the concept third, and only then demonstrate a solution if required.
Suggested 2-hour workshop pattern
Time
Mode
Purpose
0-10 min
Hook / challenge
Create curiosity and elicit prior ideas.
10-25 min
Short discovery
Introduce only the concepts needed for the task.
25-70 min
Guided + independent activity
Students use the tool or investigate the problem.
70-95 min
Test / compare / red-team
Students try to find failure, ambiguity or alternative explanations.
95-110 min
Share / discuss
Compare evidence and strategies.
110-120 min
Reflection / evidence
Capture what changed in the student’s understanding.
Student agency
Offer choice of examples, domains or project topics where possible.
Allow pairs/groups for tool-heavy work but retain individual reflection.
Use optional Level Up tasks rather than forcing the whole class to move at the speed of the fastest students.
Treat mistakes and failed models/prompts as evidence, not as failure to learn.
Inclusion and accessibility
Keep instructions in short, visible steps.
Allow evidence through writing, screenshots, diagrams or brief oral/video explanation where school policy permits.
Pair conceptual explanations with examples and visual representations.
Do not make coding a prerequisite for success.
Provide pre-prepared datasets/images where camera use or tool access is unsuitable.
Avoid requiring students to share private personal data with AI systems or the class.
AI tool rule
CORE CLASSROOM RULE
Important AI outputs are hypotheses until verified. Students remain responsible for the final judgement, explanation and work they submit.
Safety and privacy baseline
Use school-approved tools and accounts where required.
Do not ask students to enter sensitive personal information into public AI systems.
Use fictional or public scenarios for privacy/data investigations.
For image classifiers, use safe objects rather than faces unless the school explicitly approves an alternative activity.
For misinformation activities, avoid distressing or age-inappropriate synthetic media.
When a tool is unavailable, use the provided paper/simulation alternative; the learning outcome is more important than the brand of tool.
4. Assessment and portfolio model
Assessment is evidence-based and developmental rather than exam-led. Each block creates at least one portfolio artefact. The programme uses the descriptors Emerging, Developing and Extending to describe the quality and independence visible in student work.
Whole-programme descriptors
Descriptor
Typical evidence
Emerging
Completes the core task with support; identifies basic concepts; records what happened but explanation may be mostly descriptive.
Developing
Works with increasing independence; explains why something happened; uses evidence; identifies limitations and proposes sensible improvements.
Extending
Transfers learning to a new context; compares alternatives; evaluates uncertainty, bias, consequences or trade-offs; justifies decisions with evidence.

AI Innovation Project
Problem statement and user evidence; Three solution options including non-AI option; Responsible AI/data canvas; Prototype; Test record; Iteration log; Risk register; Final presentation; Individual reflection
Reflection standard
Reflections should not ask students whether they “liked” an activity. They should expose learning. Strong prompts ask what the student predicted, what evidence changed their view, what failed, what they would change, and where the concept matters outside the activity.
Feedback approach
Prefer one specific praise point + one thinking question + one next step.
Comment on the evidence or reasoning, not the student’s fixed ability.
Use peer critique with explicit criteria.
Do not reward blind use of more complex AI tools; reward appropriate tool choice and judgement.
5. Block 1 - AI & Me (2 hours)
BADGE: AI EXPLORER  |  ALIGNMENT: LO1, LO4
PURPOSE
Make AI concrete before introducing technical vocabulary. Students identify AI in familiar services, distinguish AI from ordinary automation, and begin thinking critically about influence, benefits and failure.
Student learning outcomes
Identify at least five plausible uses of AI in everyday life.
Explain, in their own words, the difference between a fixed rule and a system that learns or generates from patterns.
Identify one benefit and one possible limitation of an everyday AI system.
Form a question about how an AI system affects people.
Key concepts and vocabulary
algorithm · prediction · recommendation · pattern · automation · AI system · human oversight
Preparation and materials
Laptop/Chromebook or tablet per student/pair
Sticky notes or shared digital board
Student Mission Sheet 1
Optional access to an approved LLM for comparison activity
Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
0-10 min
Hook: AI or Not?
Show 8 examples (calculator, spam filter, playlist recommendation, traffic lights, face unlock, autocorrect, chatbot, timer). Students vote AI / not AI / unsure before definitions.
10-25 min
Mini-discovery
Facilitator introduces three useful questions: Does it use data? Does it recognise/generate patterns? Does it adapt/predict rather than follow only a fixed rule? Avoid insisting every borderline example has one perfect label.
25-50 min
AI in My Day map
Students map a typical day from waking to sleeping and mark where an algorithm may influence a choice, ranking, prediction or generated output.
50-70 min
Influence lens
For three examples students record: data in, output/decision, benefit, what could go wrong, who might be affected.
70-95 min
Human vs AI challenge
Small groups answer: “What are humans better at? What are current AI systems better at?” If an approved LLM is available, compare its list with the class list and critique both.
95-115 min
Micro-debate
Prompt: “Recommendation algorithms mostly help us.” Students place themselves on agree/unsure/disagree, justify with evidence, and may move position after discussion.
115-120 min

MISSION

6. Block 2 - Teaching a Machine (4 hours)
BADGE: MACHINE TRAINER  |  ALIGNMENT: LO1, LO2, LO3, LO9
PURPOSE
Let students experience machine learning as an experiment: create labelled data, train a simple classifier, test on unseen examples, measure errors, then discover shortcut learning and bias.
Student learning outcomes
Explain labels, features, training data, test data and model prediction.
Train a basic classifier using an approved visual tool or teacher-provided simulator.
Record predictions and calculate simple accuracy.
Interpret a basic confusion matrix.
Explain how unrepresentative training data can create poor or biased performance.
Key concepts and vocabulary
supervised learning · class label · feature · training data · test data · model · prediction · accuracy · confusion matrix · generalisation · shortcut learning · bias
Preparation and materials
Visual classifier tool such as Teachable Machine or equivalent
Two safe everyday object classes per group (e.g., cup/bottle, pen/eraser)
Camera-enabled devices if school policy permits; otherwise teacher-provided images
Test-recording sheet
Calculator/spreadsheet optional
Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
0-20 min
Human classifier
Students create a written rule-set for distinguishing two objects. Swap with another group, which tries to break the rules using unusual examples.
20-40 min
From rules to learning
Introduce training examples, labels, features and the idea that ML learns statistical patterns rather than receiving every rule explicitly.
40-75 min
Build v1 model
Groups collect balanced examples for two classes and train a simple classifier. Record dataset size and conditions.
75-100 min
Break your model
Test unseen objects, angles, lighting and backgrounds. The goal is to discover failures, not to maximise a score.
100-120 min
Evidence checkpoint
Record at least 10 tests: actual class, prediction, correct/incorrect. Calculate accuracy.
120-145 min
Confusion matrix
Convert the same results into a 2x2 matrix. Discuss which error matters more in different contexts (e.g., spam filter vs medical screening) without turning this into advanced maths.
145-175 min
Shortcut experiment
Deliberately create a flawed dataset: Class A mostly on light background, Class B mostly on dark background. Test swapped backgrounds and observe whether the model learned the object or the context.
175-210 min
Improve v2 model
Students propose and apply at least two changes: variety, balance, more examples, changed background, clearer labels. Retest using the same test set where possible.
210-230 min
Compare v1 vs v2
Groups present evidence: what changed, what improved, what remained uncertain.
230-240 min
Reflection
Explain why “high accuracy” alone may not mean a model is trustworthy.
Core student challenge
MISSION
Train a two-class model, produce a minimum 10-row test log, create a simple confusion matrix, identify one failure pattern and improve the model.
Portfolio evidence
Dataset plan
v1 test log and accuracy
Confusion matrix
Shortcut-learning observation
v2 improvement note
Reflection
Facilitator questioning
What pattern might the model be using that you did not intend?
Is your test set genuinely different from the training set?
Which mistakes matter most in this context?
How do you know improvement is real rather than luck?
Common misconceptions to surface
“The model sees an object the way a human does.”
“More examples automatically means better data.”
“Accuracy tells the whole story.”
“Bias only comes from people deliberately coding unfair rules.”
Assessment - block-specific features of quality
Descriptor
Evidence
Emerging
Trains and tests a model with support and records some results.
Developing
Uses unseen tests, calculates accuracy, explains an error pattern and improves the model.
Extending
Interprets a confusion matrix, identifies shortcut learning/bias and justifies evaluation choices.
Level Up extension
OPTIONAL CHALLENGE
Calculate precision or recall for one class and explain, in plain language, when that metric might matter more than overall accuracy.
7. Block 3 - Data Detective (3 hours)
BADGE: DATA DETECTIVE  |  ALIGNMENT: LO5, LO6, LO9
PURPOSE
Help students see data as the raw material of AI and investigate how data is collected, inferred, combined and governed. The emphasis is informed judgement, privacy and representation rather than legal memorisation.
Student learning outcomes
Distinguish volunteered, observed and inferred data.
Investigate how a digital service may collect and use data.
Explain why purpose, minimisation and representativeness matter.
Identify age-appropriate implications of GDPR and the EU AI Act.
Spot a dataset that could systematically exclude or misrepresent people.
Key concepts and vocabulary
personal data · volunteered data · observed data · inferred data · cookies · tracking · web scraping · public/private data · data minimisation · consent · GDPR · EU AI Act · representativeness
Preparation and materials

Browser access
Optional spreadsheet
Scenario cards
Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
0-15 min
Data trail warm-up
Students list what a music/video/map service can know directly and what it might infer. Sort into volunteered / observed / inferred.
15-40 min
Data Tracking Sherlock
Each student chooses one familiar service and investigates what data categories it says it collects. Keep the task at category level; students must not expose their own private data.
40-65 min
Why collect it?
For five data categories, students connect COLLECTION → PURPOSE → USER BENEFIT → POSSIBLE RISK.
65-85 min
Cookie and scraping mini-lab
Facilitator demonstrates cookies/tracking conceptually and explains web scraping using a public webpage example. Students discuss public availability versus ethical/reasonable reuse.
85-110 min
Dataset fairness challenge
Groups receive a scenario: build an AI system for school club recommendations, job shortlisting, or transport planning. They decide what data to collect and identify who may be missing.
110-135 min
Rights and safeguards
Plain-language introduction to GDPR principles relevant to students: purpose, minimisation, accuracy, security, transparency and rights. Briefly introduce risk-based AI regulation in the EU without legal detail.
135-165 min
Design a better data plan
Groups revise their scenario dataset: remove unnecessary fields, add representation checks, define retention, and specify what should require human review.
165-180 min
Reflection
“Just because data is available, does that mean it should be used?” Students justify a position with one example.

8. Block 4 - Generative AI & Prompting (4 hours)
BADGE: PROMPT ENGINEER  |  ALIGNMENT: LO7, LO9
PURPOSE
Teach students to work with generative AI as a thinking and creation tool while maintaining responsibility for goals, verification and final judgement. Prompting is taught experimentally, not as a list of “magic words”.
Student learning outcomes
Explain at a high level what an LLM does and does not do.
Improve a prompt using context, task, constraints and output format.
Compare outputs systematically instead of accepting the first response.
Use AI for explanation, ideation, critique and transformation.
Verify important factual claims and identify hallucination risk.
Key concepts and vocabulary
generative AI · large language model · token/pattern prediction (conceptual) · prompt · context · constraint · output format · iteration · hallucination · verification · source quality
Preparation and materials
School-approved generative AI tool or facilitator-run shared demonstration
Prompt Lab worksheet
Fact-check sources/browser
Scenario cards
Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
0-20 min
Same task, different prompts
Every group asks the same weak question. Compare outputs: what varies, what is missing, what looks confident but unsupported?
20-40 min
How LLMs work - useful mental model
Explain that an LLM generates likely continuations from learned patterns; it is not a database of guaranteed facts and confidence of wording is not confidence of truth.
40-70 min
Prompt Lab 1
Students improve one weak prompt using C-T-C-F: Context, Task, Constraints, Format. Save before/after outputs.
70-100 min
Prompt Lab 2 - iteration
Students add examples, audience, success criteria or a request for questions before answering. They compare which additions actually help.
100-130 min
Four productive roles
Students try AI as: tutor, brainstorm partner, critic, transformer. Each role has a different prompt pattern and a different risk.
130-160 min
Verification challenge
Give a topic with several checkable facts. Students ask AI, then independently verify three claims using reliable sources and classify supported / uncertain / wrong.
160-185 min
Prompt injection of doubt
Students deliberately ask the model leading questions and compare how framing affects responses. Discuss confirmation bias and user responsibility.
185-215 min
Create a reusable prompt
Students design a prompt template for a real TY task such as preparing for work experience, learning a topic, planning an event, or analysing survey feedback.
215-235 min
Peer red-team
Pairs try each other’s prompt and identify one ambiguity, one missing constraint and one verification need.
235-240 min
Exit rule
Write one sentence completing: “I should never trust an AI answer just because…”

MISSION

9. Block 5 - Trust, Bias & Misinformation (3 hours)
BADGE: AI INVESTIGATOR  |  ALIGNMENT: LO4, LO6, LO9
PURPOSE
Build practical information resilience. Students learn to separate fluency from reliability, identify common forms of bias, verify AI-generated claims and improve misleading content.
Student learning outcomes
Distinguish plausible language from verified evidence.
Use lateral reading and source checks to investigate claims.
Identify examples of data, selection, framing and automation bias.
Explain how generative AI can amplify misinformation.
Create a more trustworthy revision of problematic AI-generated content.
Key concepts and vocabulary
misinformation · disinformation · hallucination · bias · selection bias · representation bias · framing · automation bias · deepfake/synthetic media · provenance · lateral reading
Preparation and materials
Prepared claim cards
Browser/search access
AI News Detective worksheet
Optional approved GenAI tool
Three source-quality examples
Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
0-15 min
Confidence trap
Show three confident statements: one true, one false, one unverifiable. Students rank confidence before checking.
15-35 min
Trust checklist
Introduce SIFT-style habits in plain language: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace the claim to evidence.
35-70 min
AI News Detective
Students generate or receive a short AI-written news-style article. Highlight factual claims, emotional framing, missing sources and unsupported certainty.
70-100 min
Lateral verification
Open new tabs and verify 3-5 claims using independent reliable sources. Do not “fact-check” only by asking another chatbot.
100-125 min
Bias stations
Four short scenarios: hiring data, image generation stereotypes, school discipline analytics, recommendation feeds. Students identify where bias could enter and who could be affected.
125-150 min
Improve the output
Rewrite the news item or scenario response with verified claims, uncertainty labels, better sourcing and less loaded framing.
150-170 min
Synthetic media discussion
Discuss voice/image/video generation and why “seeing is believing” is weaker than before. Focus on verification habits, not sensational examples.
170-180 min
Reflection
Write a personal three-step rule for checking important AI-generated information.

Transfer AI literacy into useful TY study and workplace behaviours. Students practise using AI as a tutor, editor, planner and analyst while retaining authorship, judgement and accountability.
Student learning outcomes
Use AI to support understanding rather than simply produce answers.
Choose an appropriate AI role for a task.
Apply a human-review checklist to AI-assisted work.
Use spreadsheets/documents/presentations alongside AI for a small productivity task.
Explain when AI use should be disclosed.

11. Block 7 - Our AI Future (3 hours)
BADGE: FUTURE THINKER  |  ALIGNMENT: LO4, LO8
PURPOSE
Develop balanced future thinking. Students explore plausible AI futures, including AGI as a concept, without treating speculation as prediction. They connect technology choices to careers, society and responsibility.
Student learning outcomes
Distinguish current narrow/specialised AI from the idea of AGI.
Identify plausible opportunities and risks in a chosen domain.
Build more than one future scenario instead of presenting one prediction as certain.
Consider effects on jobs, skills, inclusion, sustainability and accountability.
Communicate a reasoned position about a future AI choice.
Key concepts and vocabulary
narrow AI · AGI · automation · augmentation · scenario · uncertainty · trade-off · accountability · future skills · societal impact

Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
0-20 min
2026 vs 2035
Students list AI capabilities they see now, then ideas they think might be plausible by 2035. Separate observed facts from predictions.
20-40 min
Narrow AI and AGI
Explain current specialised systems and the hypothetical idea of more general intelligence. Clarify that timelines and impacts are uncertain.
40-70 min
Career transformation map
Choose teacher, doctor, farmer, architect, engineer, journalist, tradesperson, designer, accountant or another career. Map tasks likely to be automated, augmented or still strongly human.
70-100 min
Three futures
Groups create Optimistic / Concerning / Balanced scenarios for the same domain. Each must include a technology change, human response and unintended consequence.
100-125 min
Stakeholder lens
Revisit the scenarios as worker, customer/citizen, employer, regulator and someone at risk of exclusion.
125-150 min
Policy choice debate
Choose one proposal such as “AI-generated schoolwork must always be labelled” or “High-stakes AI decisions must always have human review.” Prepare arguments and counterarguments.
150-170 min

Each student identifies three human capabilities that become more valuable in their chosen future and one action they can take during TY.
170-180 min

12. Block 8 - AI Innovation Project (8 hours)
BADGE: AI INNOVATOR  |  ALIGNMENT: LO1, LO2, LO3, LO4, LO5, LO6, LO7, LO8, LO9
PURPOSE
Integrate the entire programme through a student-chosen problem. The goal is not a polished startup or advanced code; it is evidence of problem understanding, responsible design, prototyping, testing, iteration and communication.
Student learning outcomes
Define a real user/community problem before selecting technology.
Research users and constraints without collecting unnecessary personal data.
Design and prototype an AI-enabled or AI-informed solution.
Test the prototype with clear success criteria.
Identify limitations, bias, misinformation, privacy and human-oversight risks.
Iterate based on evidence.
Present the solution, evidence and remaining uncertainties.
Key concepts and vocabulary
problem framing · user need · success criteria · prototype · testing · iteration · risk · responsible AI · human oversight · value proposition · communication

Learning sequence
Timing
Activity
Student experience / facilitator move
Hour 1
Sprint 1 - Problem Hunt
Observe school/community life. Generate at least five problems. Choose one using criteria: real, understandable, useful, testable, safe.
Hour 2
Sprint 1 - Understand the user
Define user, current process, pain points, evidence and constraints. Conduct lightweight interviews/surveys only with facilitator-approved questions and no unnecessary personal data.

Make one evidence-driven change. Red-team for hallucination, bias, privacy, misuse and overreliance. Document remaining limitations honestly.
Hour 8
Sprint 4 - Demo and reflection
3-5 minute presentation: problem, user, prototype, evidence, change made after testing, risk/safeguard, what remains uncertain. Complete final reflection.

Appendix D. Glossary
Term
Student-friendly meaning
Algorithm
A set of steps or rules used to solve a problem or produce an output.
AI model
A system trained or designed to recognise patterns, generate outputs or make predictions.
Bias
A systematic skew that can lead to unfair, unrepresentative or misleading outcomes.
Class label
The category assigned to an example in supervised learning.
Confusion matrix
A table comparing actual classes with predicted classes so different errors can be seen.
Data minimisation
Collecting only the data genuinely needed for a defined purpose.
Deep learning
Machine learning using multi-layer neural networks; introduced conceptually only in this programme.
Generative AI
AI that creates new content such as text, images, audio or code from learned patterns.
Generalisation
A model’s ability to perform sensibly on examples it did not see during training.
Hallucination
A fluent but unsupported or incorrect generated output.
Human-in-the-loop
A workflow where a person reviews, decides or approves at important points.
Inferred data
Information predicted about a person or situation from other data rather than directly provided.
Large Language Model (LLM)
A generative model trained on large amounts of text/data to predict and generate language patterns.
Machine learning
A family of methods where systems learn patterns from data rather than being given every rule explicitly.
Prompt
Instructions and context given to a generative AI system.
Shortcut learning
When a model relies on an easy but unintended pattern, such as background colour, instead of the feature we wanted.
Test data
Examples used to evaluate a model after training; ideally not reused from the training set.
Training data
Examples used to fit or teach a machine-learning model.
Verification
Checking a claim against independent, reliable evidence.
AGI
Artificial General Intelligence: a hypothetical form of AI with broad general capabilities; not the same as current specialised AI systems.
